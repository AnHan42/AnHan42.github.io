Your DNA should not have to leave the building

Posted Jan 20, 2026
FL on transcriptomic data (ICCS 2024) PP-GWAS (Nature Communications)
DNA double helix, federated genomic analysis

Genomic and transcriptomic data are arguably the most sensitive data that exist about a person, and they are sensitive in a way that most other personal data is not. A leaked password can be changed. A leaked genome cannot. It does not just describe you, it describes your relatives, whether they consented or not, and it can be used to infer ethnicity, predisposition to disease, and traits you may not even know about yourself. Transcriptomic data, which captures which genes are actively being expressed in a cell rather than the fixed genetic code itself, carries a related kind of exposure, revealing the current state of your biology rather than a fixed blueprint of it.

At the same time, this is exactly the kind of data that medical research needs in large quantities to be useful. Precision medicine, the idea of tailoring treatment to a patient’s own biomarkers and molecular profile, only works if models are trained on data from many patients across many institutions. Traditionally, that meant centralising raw genomic data in one place, a hospital network, a biobank, or a research consortium, and hoping the centre held. I find this arrangement uncomfortable, not because the researchers involved are untrustworthy, but because it puts irreversible, deeply personal information at the mercy of a single point of failure. This tension, between the scientific value of pooling data and the risk of concentrating it, is what privacy-preserving machine learning tries to resolve, and it is a problem I have spent much of my career working on.

Keeping the data where it is: federated learning

The most direct answer to “stop centralising the data” is federated learning. Instead of sending raw data anywhere, each hospital or research site trains a model locally, on its own data, and shares only the resulting model updates with a coordinating server, which combines everyone’s updates into a shared model. The raw genomes or transcriptomes never leave the building they were collected in.

The appeal is obvious: it directly addresses the biggest source of risk, a large centralised store of raw genetic data, without asking institutions to give up on collaborative research. In my own work applying this to real transcriptomic data, the model quality on offer was genuinely encouraging: federating the training did not meaningfully hold the models back compared to training on pooled data, at least when a reasonable number of institutions took part.

The pitfall is more subtle than “does it work.” Federated learning protects against a specific threat: a coordinating server that follows the rules but would look at raw data if it could. It does not protect against a server that secretly deviates from the protocol, or against a determined adversary trying to reconstruct individual records from the aggregated updates it receives. And it has diminishing returns of its own: spread the same total amount of data across too many participating sites, and each institution ends up training on such a thin slice of the problem that the shared model quietly loses the very benefit of scale that motivated pooling data in the first place. Federated learning is a genuine improvement, not a complete solution.

Adding a mathematical guarantee: differential privacy

Because federated learning alone does not stop someone from working backward from the aggregated updates to individual records, it is often paired with differential privacy: adding carefully calibrated random noise to what each site shares, so that no single participant’s data can be confidently reconstructed from the result. This gives you something federated learning alone does not, a formal, mathematical guarantee about how much any one person’s data could have influenced the outcome.

The pitfall here surprised me the most in practice. I expected the trade-off between privacy and model quality to be gradual: a little more noise, a little less accuracy, a smooth curve you could tune to taste. It is not. Model quality tends to hold up reasonably well until the noise crosses a threshold needed for meaningful protection, and then it does not degrade, it collapses, becoming close to useless almost immediately after. There is very little room to negotiate between “private enough to matter” and “still a working model.” That means differential privacy cannot be treated as a dial you turn up until it feels responsible. You have to know, for your specific model and data, where the cliff actually is before deciding whether privacy and usefulness can coexist at all.

Hiding the computation itself: homomorphic encryption

A structurally different answer is to encrypt the data before it ever gets exchanged, using homomorphic encryption, which allows computations to be performed directly on encrypted values without ever decrypting them. Applied to something like a genome-wide association study, this means the server coordinating the analysis across hospitals never sees anything but ciphertext, and can still compute the statistics needed to find genetic associations. A project I contributed to, PP-GWAS, led by my colleague Arjhun Swaminathan, takes this approach, combining it with federated computation so that even the intermediate values exchanged during the study stay encrypted rather than merely aggregated.

The benefit is a stronger guarantee than federated learning or differential privacy offer on their own: the coordinating server genuinely cannot see individual contributions, not just a noised version of them. The pitfall is cost. Homomorphic encryption is computationally expensive, and coordinating it across hospital systems with different infrastructure adds real complexity. It is a trade worth making for data as sensitive as a genome, but it is not a trade you get for free, and it is not the right tool for every problem.

What none of these solve

All three approaches share a blind spot: they are technical answers to a problem that is not only technical. None of them substitute for meaningful informed consent. A patient can understand, correctly, that their data never left the hospital and was never seen unencrypted, and still have no idea what the study is trying to learn, how long their data will be retained, or who benefits from the research. Consent, retention, and benefit-sharing are governance questions, and no amount of cryptography answers them on its own.

Why this matters beyond genomics

I think the genomics case is useful precisely because it is extreme. If we can build privacy-preserving infrastructure for data this sensitive, we have a working template for a much wider class of problems: medical imaging, electronic health records, financial data, location histories. The pattern repeats each time. Valuable research requires data from many people, centralising that data creates risk, and privacy-preserving techniques can decouple the scientific value from at least part of that risk, provided you are honest about which threats they actually cover and which they leave standing.

The technical pieces exist. What we need now is the will to build systems around them, and the governance frameworks to make sure those systems are used responsibly. That last part is what brought me to SCRAI. The engineering is, in some ways, the easier half of the problem.
